Context and problem

Merchants and finance teams use reports to reconcile sales, check what was refunded and know what they will be paid. Large chains filter across many merchants and stores. Small merchants usually want yesterday’s total and nothing else. When a payment is questioned, they need the full story right away: who paid, how, what fees applied and what happened next.

The old reports P14 sample hid key filters and returned tables that were hard to scan. Transaction information was P59 sample spread across several views.

Constraints

  • Reporting back-end limits: P22 sample large reports can take up to 20 seconds to generate
  • Merchants who handle both CAD and USD
  • Dozens of transaction fields, many needed for disputes and reconciliation
  • Cardholder numbers must be masked, for example 4242****4242. P29 sample Full card numbers may never be shown or exported.
  • No design system for tables, filters or date pickers

Key decisions: Sales Summary

Filter first, then preview

What I chose
The report starts with one filter screen: merchant or chain, card type, date range and Group by, with Preview and Reset.
Why
Merchants shape the report before they wait for it.
What it cost
P23 sample one extra step for simple needs, so I added defaults

Always show which account and dates

What I chose
Account number, account name and date range sit at the top of every summary.
Why
P24 sample chain users switch accounts constantly and reading the wrong account is costly
What it cost
Uses vertical space on small screens.

One table per currency, with a clear No Data state

What I chose
CAD and USD are separated. An empty table says so instead of leaving a gap.
Why
Mixing currencies makes totals hard to trust.
What it cost
Merchants with one currency see one table, so the layout adapts.

Totals that stand out

What I chose
The grand total row is visually distinct from line items.
Why
Totals are what people reconcile against.
What it cost
None worth noting.

Three time views

What I chose
Summary for the whole range, monthly summary and daily summary.
Why
Owners and finance teams think in different time units.
What it cost
More views to maintain, so they share one table component.

Export where people look

What I chose
Save as PDF and download actions sit beside the data.
Why
Reports are shared with accountants and owners.
What it cost
None worth noting.

The decision I’m proudest of: page or modal?

I did not pick the first idea. I designed two full options for transaction details, a full page and a modal, and compared them against written criteria: P30 sample data volume, the need to compare transactions, and small screens. I chose P31 sample the full page, because merchants compare transactions and need room for dense fee data, aligned with P32 sample the product manager and two developers.

A summary strip first

What I chose
Merchant number, date and time, card type, masked cardholder number, transaction type and amount sit at the top.
Why
P33 sample it answers the most common questions without scrolling
What it cost
Fields in the strip had to be chosen ruthlessly.

Sections that match how people think

What I chose
General, Fees, Gateway and Associated transactions, with a sticky side navigation that highlights the current section.
Why
Merchants arrive with a specific question, such as why they were charged a fee. Long pages need orientation.
What it cost
P36 sample a single flat view of every field

Fee transparency

What I chose
A fee table shows each charge with description, rate, flat amount and total, under a header naming the fee method.
Why
Fees are a top source of confusion. P34 sample In the tickets I reviewed, about 30% were about fees.
What it cost
Adds length for transactions with many fees.

A deliberate Refund action

What I chose
Refund is the primary action in brand yellow. Save as PDF and View receipt are secondary.
Why
Refund is the most consequential action, so it must be findable but not accidental. P35 sample Clicking Refund opens a confirmation step with the amount and a reason.
What it cost
One extra click to refund.

Associated transactions together

What I chose
Related payments, refunds and adjustments are listed on the same page.
Why
People want the full chain without searching.
What it cost
Needs an extra query from the back end.

Fraud detection

Refund abuse and unusual patterns are real losses for merchants, and finding them usually meant exporting data and checking by hand. P40 sample Merchants exported data and checked it by hand. Status: in pilot.

A guided report builder

What I chose
Pick a report type, set the rules, choose the account and date range.
Why
Merchants answer a few questions instead of building a query.
What it cost
Less flexible than free-form filters.

Rules in plain language

What I chose
Checkbox rules such as number of refunds at or above a value between 2 and 999, or total refunds exceeding total purchases.
Why
Merchants should not need fraud-analyst vocabulary.
What it cost
P43 sample Analysts set the thresholds and developers confirmed feasibility

A fixed Reset and Apply footer

What I chose
Actions stay in view while the form scrolls.
Why
Merchants can adjust and re-run quickly.
What it cost
Takes a little screen height.

A home in the main navigation

What I chose
Fraud detection sits next to the other reports.
Why
P42 sample a feature nobody finds does not exist
What it cost
None worth noting.

P44 sample Analysts wanted twenty rule options and developers could support six. I tested a short and a long form with merchants, and the short one was finished far faster. We launched with three rules and a way to request more, which kept the form simple and kept the analysts on board.

Accessibility

P25 sample contrast checks on every state, a logical keyboard order through the filters, table headers tied to cells, and errors that do not rely on colour

Results

P26 sample Report completion time fell 41%, from 148 to 87 seconds, measured on usertesting.com with the same task before and after.
