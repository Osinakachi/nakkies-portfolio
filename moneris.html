Moneris Go Portal

I redesigned the portal merchants use to run their payments, from one-store shops to national chains. P13 sample Merchants now reach their sales totals in 3 steps instead of 7.

Company
Moneris, one of Canada’s largest payment processors
My role
Senior Product Designer. Lead designer on some features, collaborating with other designers on others, and sole designer on the rest
Team
Product managers, analysts, developers and fellow designers
Timeline
About one year. P1 sample Four phases over 12 months.
Scope
Navigation and information architecture, reports, transaction details, fraud detection, invoicing, Virtual Terminal, recurring payments, Vault, Visa Installments, terminal settings, sign-in and design system
Methods
usertesting.com sessions, client interviews, support ticket analysis, journey mapping, prototyping, accessibility review (WCAG 2.1 AA)
Tools
Figma, FigJam, usertesting.com. P58 sample Jira, Confluence and Miro.

The challenge

Merchants open this portal to answer the questions that matter to a business. How much did I sell? Where is my money? Was this payment legitimate? Can I take a payment over the phone? A single-location café and a national chain use the same product with the same urgency, but with very different needs and very different payments knowledge.

When I started, the portal had grown one feature at a time. P2 sample Screens had been built by different teams at different times, with different patterns. The result for merchants was P3 sample they had to relearn each page, and support heard the same questions again and again.

Constraints I designed within

No shared design system existed. Merchants range from one-store shops to large chains. Payments data is dense and must be masked and handled carefully. Everything had to meet WCAG 2.1 AA. Whether I led or worked alongside other designers, every decision had to be explained to product, analysts and developers, not just drawn.

My approach

I treated this as a product problem with a design-system answer. The plan had three parts.

  • Listen first. Learn what merchants were trying to do through usertesting.com sessions, client interviews and support tickets, before drawing screens.
  • Fix the highest-friction flows first. I started with reports and transaction details because P4 sample they had the highest usage and the most support tickets.
  • Build the system as I go. Every redesigned screen fed a shared component library, so each next screen was faster, more consistent and accessible by default.

How I work

  1. Discover

    Moderated and unmoderated sessions on usertesting.com, interviews with clients, and tagging support tickets to see where merchants got stuck.

  2. Define

    Turned findings into problem statements and journeys, and agreed priorities with product managers and analysts.

  3. Design

    Explored options in Figma, compared them against written criteria, and documented the trade-offs so decisions could be revisited.

  4. Validate

    Prototype tests with merchants, accessibility checks against WCAG 2.1 AA, and reviews with developers on feasibility.

  5. Deliver

    Specs, component documentation and build reviews with developers, then follow-up after release.

P51 sample I joined developers in weekly build reviews, kept one shared component doc, and sat in QA before every release.

What I learned
